---
title: "Hitter Training: Match Your Bat to the Pitch Plane"
sidebarTitle: "Hitter Training"
description: "Build a repeatable swing by matching your bat path to the plane of the pitch — the same approach that transformed J.D. Martinez into an elite hitter."
---

Every elite hitter shares one mechanical truth: the bat must travel *on* the plane of the incoming pitch, not across it. This is the core of the swing-plane philosophy developed by hitting coaches Craig Wallenbrock and Robert Van Scoyoc — the same approach J.D. Martinez credited with turning him from a released minor leaguer into one of the most feared hitters in baseball. Whether you're stepping into the cage for the first time or logging your hundredth filmed session, every drill on this page is designed to groove that plane-matching habit so contact becomes consistent, exit velocity climbs, and hard line drives stop feeling like accidents.

<Note>
  **Equipment overview:** A batting tee is sufficient for Beginner and Developing levels. Intermediate and Advanced work adds a pitching machine. Advanced rotational power work requires a medicine ball (6–10 lb). Always wear a batting helmet during any live-ball drill.
</Note>

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Beginner">
    At the Beginner level the goal is simple: make contact, stay balanced, and have fun in the cage. You're building the raw feel of a swing — a relaxed grip, a balanced finish, and the confidence that comes from consistently putting wood on ball. Keep sessions short (20–30 minutes), praise solid contact over power, and don't overload technique.

    <Accordion title="Tee Work — Belt-Height Contact Drill">
      **Purpose:** Establish a balanced, repeatable swing at the most hittable pitch location.

      **Setup:** Place a batting tee at true belt height (navel level) over the middle of home plate. Use a standard batting tee and a bucket of 10–15 balls.

      **Steps:**
      1. Set your feet shoulder-width apart, slight bend in the knees, weight evenly distributed.
      2. Hold the bat with a relaxed grip — no white knuckles. Hands stay near the back shoulder.
      3. Take a small, controlled stride (4–6 inches) toward the pitcher, landing softly on the ball of your front foot.
      4. Fire your hips toward the ball *before* your hands move. Feel the hips lead.
      5. Drive the barrel through the ball and finish with your hands high — belt buckle facing the pitcher, back foot on its toe.
      6. Hold your finish for one full second before stepping out. Check: are you balanced? Did your head stay down?

      **Reps:** 3 sets of 10 swings. Rest 60 seconds between sets.

      <Note>If you're losing your balance at the finish, widen your stance by one inch at a time until you feel stable. Don't rush the fix.</Note>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Soft-Toss Game — Count the Line Drives">
      **Purpose:** Turn tee fundamentals into a reactive, game-like drill while keeping it fun.

      **Setup:** A feeder kneels 10–12 feet to the hitter's front-and-side, tossing underhand to the hitting zone. Use a fence or net as a target. Mark a "line-drive zone" on the net — roughly waist-to-shoulder height at the target.

      **Steps:**
      1. Feeder calls "ready," pauses one beat, then tosses a soft underhand pitch belt-high.
      2. The hitter tracks the ball from the feeder's hand all the way to contact — eyes on the ball, not the net.
      3. After each swing, both the hitter and feeder call out whether it was a line drive (hits the marked zone), a ground ball, or a pop-up.
      4. Keep a running tally out loud: "That's 3 line drives out of 5!"
      5. Challenge: try to hit 7 line drives out of 10 tosses before moving to the next round.

      **Reps:** 3 rounds of 10 tosses. Short rest between rounds.

      <Note>The point of counting is to make the hitter self-aware — not to shame misses. Celebrate every line drive genuinely.</Note>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Front Toss — Live Tracking Introduction">
      **Purpose:** Introduce the feel of a ball coming directly at you from a short distance, building tracking habits and timing.

      **Setup:** Feeder sits or kneels behind an L-screen 20–25 feet away, tossing overhand (or underhand) straight at the strike zone. Keep velocity very low — just enough arc for the hitter to track comfortably.

      **Steps:**
      1. The hitter sets up in their normal stance, same as tee work.
      2. The feeder tosses one ball at a time, calling "pitch" as it leaves their hand.
      3. The hitter watches the ball all the way in. If it's a strike, swing. If it's clearly off the plate, take it — start building plate discipline habits early.
      4. After every 5 pitches, pause and talk through one thing: "Were you watching the ball all the way to the bat?"
      5. Focus is on timing and contact — not power.

      **Reps:** 3 sets of 8–10 pitches.

      <Warning>Always use an L-screen to protect the feeder. Even low-velocity contact can redirect a ball back at speed.</Warning>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Simple Video Check — One-Thought Review">
      **Purpose:** Give the hitter a visual reference without overwhelming them with information.

      **Setup:** Use a phone or tablet on a tripod, set up directly behind the hitter (down-the-line view), recording at normal speed.

      **Steps:**
      1. Film one full set of 10 tee swings.
      2. Watch the video together. Look for only **one thing**: does the hitter finish in a balanced position with their belt buckle facing the pitcher?
      3. Pick one swing that looked great. Watch it twice. Say, "See that? That's your swing."
      4. Pick one swing that needed work. Identify only the finish position — not multiple flaws.
      5. Hit another set of 10 with only the finish thought in mind, then film again and compare.

      **Frequency:** Once every 2–3 sessions.

      <Note>At the Beginner level, resist the urge to fix everything on video. One positive, one correction, done. More than that creates paralysis.</Note>
    </Accordion>
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Developing">
    At the Developing level you've built basic contact and balance. Now it's time to understand *where* the pitch is and adjust your swing path accordingly. You'll work at multiple tee heights, respond to verbal pitch-location calls, and use video to compare your path on low pitches versus high pitches.

    <Accordion title="Multi-Height Tee Work — Low, Mid, High Adjustment">
      **Purpose:** Train your body to feel the path change required for different pitch heights.

      **Setup:** Set up three tee positions in sequence:
      - **Low:** bottom of the strike zone (top of the knee)
      - **Mid:** belt height (navel)
      - **High:** top of the strike zone (letters)

      **Steps:**
      1. Start with **10 reps at low**. To hit the low pitch properly, you must drop your back shoulder slightly and extend your arms more — the barrel has to stay on the pitch's downward plane longer. Don't pull your hands to the ball; let the barrel stay deep and drive up through the zone.
      2. Move to **10 reps at mid**. This is your home-base swing — balanced shoulder tilt, direct path.
      3. Move to **10 reps at high**. On the high pitch, the barrel enters slightly flatter and the contact point moves out front more. Keep your hands inside the ball.
      4. After all three blocks, reset to low and do one more round — can you feel the difference without thinking about it?

      **Reps:** 3 full rotations (Low → Mid → High), 10 reps per height.

      <Note>If you're rolling over or hitting weak grounders on low pitches, the barrel is dropping too early. Try the "stay-through" cue: keep the barrel in the zone for an extra six inches after contact.</Note>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Front Toss with Call-Outs — 'High' or 'Low'">
      **Purpose:** React to pitch location in real time, linking verbal information to a physical adjustment.

      **Setup:** Feeder behind L-screen, 20–25 feet away. Before each toss, the feeder calls either "high" or "low" — then delivers to that location.

      **Steps:**
      1. Hitter hears the call, sets up mentally for that pitch height, and initiates their load.
      2. The goal is a conscious path adjustment *before* the ball arrives — not a reactive flail.
      3. After each swing, the feeder gives binary feedback: "Path matched" or "Path didn't match." Keep it simple.
      4. Do a block of 10 with call-outs, then immediately do a block of 10 with **no** call-outs (see next drill). Compare the feel.

      **Reps:** 3 sets of 10 (with call-outs).
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Mixed-Height Front Toss — No Call-Outs">
      **Purpose:** Force your eyes to read pitch height and trigger the correct path adjustment without verbal help — closer to real at-bat conditions.

      **Setup:** Same as call-out drill, but feeder gives no verbal cue. Pitches are randomly thrown to different heights within the strike zone.

      **Steps:**
      1. Load normally. Read the pitch height as early as possible — try to see it within the first 10 feet of flight.
      2. Make the path adjustment naturally. If you're reacting too late, slow the toss down temporarily until your read-and-adjust timing improves, then gradually increase velocity.
      3. After every 10 pitches, take a 60-second break and think through two pitches you remember: what height were they, and did your path match?

      **Reps:** 3 sets of 10.

      <Note>Most hitters react too late at first on this drill. That's normal and expected. The read window will tighten with repetition over several sessions.</Note>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Swing Video Review — Path Comparison">
      **Purpose:** Visually confirm that your swing path actually changes between low, mid, and high pitches.

      **Setup:** Film from the **pitcher's-side angle** (directly in front of the hitter, off to the side) and from **behind (down-the-line)**. Record one full set at each tee height.

      **Steps:**
      1. Watch the low-pitch clip and pause just before contact. Where is the barrel relative to your hands? It should be below your hands, driving up through the zone.
      2. Watch the high-pitch clip at the same moment. The barrel should be more even with your hands, approaching more level.
      3. If both clips look identical, your path isn't adjusting — go back to multi-height tee work for another session before returning to front toss.
      4. Write down one word that describes each swing after watching. "Long," "short," "steep," "flat" — just one word per clip. This builds self-awareness.

      **Frequency:** Every 2–3 sessions.
    </Accordion>
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Intermediate">
    At the Intermediate level you understand the concept of swing plane. Now the work is about drilling it under increasing demand — more reps per height, machine pitching, situational at-bats, and consistent use of film to catch path drift before it becomes habit.

    <Accordion title="Plane-Matching Tee Ladder — 10 Reps per Height, Filmed">
      **Purpose:** Build a high-volume groove at all three pitch heights while creating a film record to track path consistency over time.

      **Setup:** Three tee positions (low, mid, high) as in Developing level. Camera set up on a tripod at the pitcher's-side angle. Film every third session in full.

      **Steps:**
      1. Complete **10 reps at low**, focusing on a barrel entry that matches the pitch's downward angle — approximately 6–10 degrees of upward barrel travel through the zone.
      2. Rest 90 seconds. Complete **10 reps at mid**. Barrel travel roughly 4–6 degrees upward.
      3. Rest 90 seconds. Complete **10 reps at high**. Near-level barrel path, contact slightly out front.
      4. On filmed sessions: watch each block back-to-back. Are the barrel paths visibly different per height? If not, you're defaulting to one swing for all pitches.
      5. Log a one-line note after each filmed session: e.g., "Low plane looked good, high pitch still too steep."

      **Reps:** 3 full ladders (Low → Mid → High), 10 reps each. Filmed every 3rd session.
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Dry-Swing Plane Drill — Pause and Check Entry Angle">
      **Purpose:** Create a conscious "feel check" at the key moment of your swing — the entry of the barrel into the hitting zone — without a ball present.

      **Setup:** No ball, no tee. Stand in your normal stance at the plate. Have a partner or mirror available if possible.

      **Steps:**
      1. Take your normal load and stride.
      2. Initiate your swing and **stop the bat** at the moment the barrel would enter the hitting zone — roughly when your hands pass your front hip.
      3. Freeze. Check: is the barrel below your hands, angled upward to match a pitch coming downward? Or is the barrel above your hands, chopping down?
      4. Hold for 3 seconds. Feel that position. Reset.
      5. Do 10 paused reps, then 10 full-speed reps trying to reproduce the same entry angle from the pause position.

      **Reps:** 3 sets of 10 paused + 10 full-speed.

      <Note>If you can't feel the difference between a correct and incorrect entry angle, have a partner place a hand where the ball would be — swing to their hand and pause. Their hand gives you a tactile reference point.</Note>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Machine Reps — Pitching by Height">
      **Purpose:** Apply your plane-matching mechanics to a consistent, machine-delivered pitch at each height zone.

      **Setup:** Pitching machine set to age-appropriate velocity (typically 50–65 mph for Intermediate). Set the machine to deliver exclusively to one height zone per round. Use a standard safety batting cage and wear a helmet.

      **Steps:**
      1. Round 1: Machine set to **low** zone, 10–12 pitches. Focus: early barrel entry, drive up through the zone.
      2. Rest 2 minutes. Round 2: Machine set to **mid** zone, 10–12 pitches. Focus: balanced path, full hip rotation.
      3. Rest 2 minutes. Round 3: Machine set to **high** zone, 10–12 pitches. Focus: contact slightly out front, stay through the ball.
      4. Optional Round 4: Machine alternates between heights randomly. Do not try to out-think it — react with your eyes.

      **Reps:** 3 rounds per height, 10–12 pitches each.

      <Warning>Never enter or approach a pitching machine while it is loaded or spinning. Always have a coach or adult present when operating the machine. Keep all bystanders behind the batter's eye line.</Warning>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Situational Rounds — Two-Strike and Opposite Field">
      **Purpose:** Practice applying your swing plane under at-bat pressure — specifically the two scenarios that most often expose mechanical flaws.

      **Setup:** Front toss or machine, strike zone pitches only. Simulate a 2-strike count mentally before the round begins.

      **Two-Strike Round:**
      1. Tell yourself the count is 0-2 before the first pitch. Your job is to protect the plate and put the ball in play — not to pull for power.
      2. Choke up one inch on the bat.
      3. Expand your zone slightly — swing at anything close.
      4. Focus on contact quality, not exit velocity. Line drives and hard grounders count.
      5. 10 pitches, 2-strike mindset throughout.

      **Opposite-Field Round:**
      1. Set a target zone on the opposite-field side of the cage or net.
      2. Let the ball travel deeper into the zone (closer to the back hip) before swinging.
      3. Resist the urge to pull — feel the barrel stay through the ball and drive it the other way.
      4. 10 pitches, opposite-field target throughout.

      **Reps:** 2 rounds of each (two-strike, opposite-field) per session.
    </Accordion>
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Advanced">
    Advanced hitters have solid plane-matching mechanics and enough self-awareness to maintain them under volume and pressure. The focus now is on logging data, increasing intensity, building rotational power, and sharpening your ability to execute in specific game situations without mechanical breakdown.

    <Accordion title="Recorded Swing Sessions — Film Every Session, Log Changes">
      **Purpose:** Build a consistent visual record of your swing over weeks and months so you can identify drift, track improvements, and self-correct before bad habits compound.

      **Setup:** Camera on tripod, pitcher's-side angle, every session. Log kept in a notebook or notes app.

      **Steps:**
      1. Film every hitting session — tee work, front toss, and machine reps.
      2. After each session, watch 5–6 representative swings and log three data points:
         - **Barrel entry angle** (does it match the drill's pitch height?)
         - **Hip-to-hands sequencing** (do hips clear before hands move?)
         - **Finish position** (balanced, belt buckle forward, back toe?)
      3. Write one sentence describing today's session: "Low-pitch plane was consistent, pulled off on high pitches" or "Best session in two weeks — hip lead was sharp all day."
      4. Review the past three session logs before starting each new session. Are you seeing a pattern? Correct it in warm-up, not in the middle of reps.

      **Frequency:** Every session.

      <Note>Keep a consistent camera position across all sessions so comparisons are valid. Mark the tripod spot on the floor with tape if you train in the same facility regularly.</Note>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="High-Volume Varied-Height BP — Tee, Front Toss, and Machine">
      **Purpose:** Build swing-plane durability by maintaining mechanical quality across a high-rep session that moves through all three delivery methods.

      **Setup:** Plan a structured session block. Total volume: 90–120 swings. Break it into three phases.

      **Phase 1 — Tee (30 swings):**
      - 10 low, 10 mid, 10 high. Film this block.

      **Phase 2 — Front Toss (30 swings):**
      - Mixed heights, no call-outs. Feeder tracks error patterns.

      **Phase 3 — Machine (30–60 swings):**
      - First 15: single height per round.
      - Last 15–45: random height, full game-speed pitch velocity.

      **Between phases:** 3-minute rest, review one film clip, reset mentally.

      **Reps:** Full session 2–3 times per week. Don't exceed 3 high-volume sessions per week — quality degrades past that.

      <Warning>High swing volume increases forearm and wrist fatigue. If you notice grip strength fading significantly in Phase 3, end the session. Swinging with compromised grip reinforces bad mechanics and risks injury.</Warning>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Rotational Power Work — Medicine Ball Series (3×8 per side)">
      **Purpose:** Develop the hip and core rotational force that drives bat speed, using medicine ball throws as a training analog to the swing.

      **Equipment:** 6–10 lb medicine ball, solid wall or rebounder.

      **Exercise 1 — Rotational Wall Throw:**
      1. Stand sideways to a solid wall, 3–4 feet away, in your batting stance.
      2. Load your back hip (mirror your swing load) and drive the ball into the wall with a full hip-and-core rotation.
      3. Catch the rebound and reset. Move through the full range of motion — don't arm-toss.
      4. 3 sets of 8 reps per side.

      **Exercise 2 — Hip-Load Step-and-Throw:**
      1. Start facing away from the wall, feet together.
      2. Step and pivot into your stance (like a stride), load your back hip, then throw rotationally into the wall.
      3. This mimics the stride-to-rotation sequence of your swing.
      4. 3 sets of 8 reps per side.

      **Exercise 3 — Overhead Slam (core stability):**
      1. Stand facing the wall, feet shoulder-width.
      2. Raise ball overhead, brace your core, and slam down into the floor or against the wall.
      3. 3 sets of 8 reps.

      **Rest:** 60–90 seconds between sets.

      <Note>Medicine ball work is a supplement to hitting, not a replacement. Do rotational work 2–3 times per week on hitting days, after your swing session — never before, as pre-fatigue affects mechanics.</Note>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Two-Strike and Situational Work — Advanced Execution">
      **Purpose:** Execute your best swing mechanics in the most pressure-relevant game scenarios, combining technical discipline with competitive intent.

      **Setup:** Machine or live front toss. Coach or partner tracks and calls situations aloud.

      **Two-Strike Survival Round:**
      1. 0-2 count called before the pitch. Choke up, shorten, protect.
      2. Goal: 8 of 10 pitches put in play (no strikeouts). Track and log your success rate week-to-week.
      3. Grade each contact: line drive = A, hard groundball = B, weak contact = C, strikeout = F.

      **Opposite-Field Power Round:**
      1. Machine set to outer third of the plate. Task: drive the ball to the opposite field with authority — not a flick, a real barrel turn.
      2. Focus on the hip-to-hands sequence staying intact even as you let the ball travel deep.
      3. 10 pitches, log the number of true opposite-field line drives.

      **Runner-On-Base Situation Round:**
      1. Coach calls the situation: "Runner on second, one out — go the other way." Or "Runner on first, nobody out — hit it to the right side."
      2. Hitter must execute the situational task, not just swing freely.
      3. 3 different situations per session, 5–7 pitches each.

      **Reps:** One full situational block per session (25–30 pitches total).
    </Accordion>
