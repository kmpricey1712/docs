---
title: "Base Running Drills: Read the Field and Score More Runs"
sidebarTitle: "Base Running"
description: "Master pro-level base-running fundamentals — disciplined secondary leads, sharp reads on batted balls, and the mechanics to turn singles into doubles."
---

Base running is the most underpracticed skill in amateur baseball, and it's one of the most direct ways to manufacture runs without the bat. Every professional organization drills the same core principles: run at full speed through the bag, take a disciplined secondary lead at the right moment, and read every batted ball correctly to make the right send-or-hold decision. Mistakes on the bases are momentum killers — a runner thrown out at third in the second inning can change the entire shape of a game. The drills on this page build the habits and reads that separate smart, aggressive baserunners from passive ones who wait to be waved.

<Note>
  **Before every base-running session:** Do a 5–10 minute dynamic warm-up — leg swings, high knees, lateral shuffles, and two build-up sprints at 75–85% effort. Base-running work puts explosive demand on your hamstrings and hip flexors. Cold muscles and sprint work don't mix.
</Note>

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Beginner">
    At the Beginner level, the priority is building three instincts that most young players get wrong: run *through* the bag (not to it), shuffle *at* pitcher release (not early), and hit the inside corner of the bag when rounding. These feel small, but they're the foundation everything else is built on.

    <Accordion title="Run-Through-the-Bag — Full-Speed Reps">
      **Purpose:** Eliminate the habit of decelerating before first base by building the physical and mental pattern of running *through* the bag at maximum effort.

      **Setup:** Home plate to first base, full distance (90 feet). Use actual bases if available; cones work as a substitute.

      **Steps:**
      1. Start in your batter's-box position. Simulate a swing (full follow-through) before breaking for first.
      2. The instant your swing finishes, drive hard with your back leg — first step is explosive, not a jog.
      3. Run a straight line from the batter's box to first base. Don't veer left or right.
      4. Hit the front edge of the bag — the side facing home plate — and keep running at full speed for 5–8 steps past the bag.
      5. After passing the bag, turn to your **right** (toward foul territory). This keeps you from being in a position to advance to second unless you've overrun it.
      6. Walk back to the start. Repeat.

      **Key coaching cue:** "Don't look for the bag — feel it with your foot and run through it."

      **Reps:** 8–10 full-speed reps. Full rest between each.

      <Warning>Never turn left (toward the field of play) after overrunning first base unless you are actively continuing to second. Turning left is treated by umpires as an attempt to advance and you can be tagged out.</Warning>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Secondary Lead Basics — Shuffle at Pitcher Release">
      **Purpose:** Learn the correct timing of the secondary lead so you're moving at the moment of pitcher release — maximizing your jump without getting picked off.

      **Setup:** A base at first, a partner standing on a pitching mound or a marked spot 60 feet away simulating a pitcher.

      **Steps:**
      1. Take your primary lead — 2–3 comfortable walking steps off the bag. Weight on the balls of your feet, slight athletic bend in the knees.
      2. Watch the "pitcher" (your partner). The moment their arm breaks toward home (simulated release), take **two shuffle steps** toward second.
      3. A shuffle step is not a sprint — it's a lateral, controlled hop-step. Left foot moves toward second, right foot follows. Stay low, stay balanced.
      4. If the ball is hit, you're already moving. If the ball is not hit (in this drill), simply return to first.
      5. Practice the **timing** — two shuffles as release happens, not before. Early shuffles kill your ability to get back on a pickoff.

      **Reps:** 15–20 secondary lead timing reps. Partner can "fake" the release occasionally to check if the runner goes early.

      <Note>A good secondary lead is measured in timing, not distance. Two perfectly timed shuffle steps beat three early ones every time.</Note>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Rounding First Base — Inside Corner, Eyes to Second">
      **Purpose:** Practice the proper arc and foot placement for rounding first base so you can look toward second without breaking stride.

      **Setup:** Full 90-foot run from home to first. Mark the "banana arc" starting point — about 3–4 feet into foul territory, beginning roughly 15–20 feet before first base.

      **Steps:**
      1. Start at home and break for first as if you've hit a ball into the outfield gap.
      2. At about the 60-foot mark, begin a gradual arc into foul territory — not a sharp cut. Think banana curve, not a right-angle turn.
      3. Hit the **inside corner** of first base with your left foot (or whichever foot naturally lands). This allows your body's momentum to carry you toward second without a wasted step.
      4. As you touch the bag and round past it, turn your head and look directly toward second base. What do you see? Where is the ball?
      5. If you're in a drill environment with no live ball, practice the look-and-read motion regardless. Build the habit of always looking.
      6. Sprint 10–15 feet past first in the direction of second, then stop.

      **Reps:** 8 reps at 90% speed, 2 reps at full game speed.
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Watch-and-React — Coach Rolls Ground Balls">
      **Purpose:** Learn to react to a batted ball as a baserunner, starting the process of reading ground balls versus fly balls and deciding whether to run.

      **Setup:** Runner stands on first base in their secondary-lead position. Coach stands at home plate with a bucket of balls and rolls or tosses ground balls in different directions.

      **Steps:**
      1. Runner takes a primary lead. Coach signals "set" — runner takes their secondary lead shuffles at the moment of the simulated pitch.
      2. Coach rolls a ground ball: sometimes straight up the middle, sometimes to the right side (toward first/second), sometimes to the shortstop hole.
      3. Runner's job: read the ball off the ground and decide "go" or "hold."
         - **Ground ball to the right side** (second baseman or first baseman moving away): go, your path is clear.
         - **Ground ball directly at the shortstop or third baseman**: hold — it may be caught and turned into a force out.
      4. Coach gives feedback immediately: "right read" or "wrong read." No punishment for wrong reads at this level — just immediate correction.

      **Reps:** 20 reads total. Mix directions.

      <Note>At the Beginner level, the goal is simply to understand that *not all ground balls are automatic runs*. The read-and-decision habit will be refined at every level after this.</Note>
    </Accordion>
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Developing">
    At the Developing level you understand the basics of the secondary lead and reading the ball. Now you'll time your secondary lead with a stopwatch, develop your first-step reaction to different ball types, and start reading live batted balls from first base during soft-toss or live BP situations.

    <Accordion title="Timed Secondary Lead — Shuffle at Release, Explode on Play">
      **Purpose:** Add accountability to your secondary lead by measuring the time from the moment of pitch to your first full stride toward second on a batted ball.

      **Setup:** Runner at first, partner with a stopwatch, partner at home simulating a pitch and hitting/rolling a ball.

      **Steps:**
      1. Runner takes primary lead, then secondary shuffles as the simulated pitch is delivered.
      2. The moment the ball is "hit" (partner taps or rolls ball), the stopwatch starts.
      3. The stopwatch stops when the runner's right foot hits the ground on their **first full stride toward second**.
      4. Target time: under 0.45 seconds from ball contact to first stride.
      5. If your time is over 0.50 seconds, you're either shuffling too early (and stopping) or not driving with your back leg on the break. Diagnose and fix.

      **Reps:** 12–15 timed reps. Log times.

      <Note>This drill measures first-step quickness, not overall speed. A fast, decisive first step is more valuable than raw sprint speed on the basepaths in most situations.</Note>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="First-Step Reaction Drill — Partner Calls 'Ground' or 'Fly'">
      **Purpose:** Build an automatic, correct first-step response to ball type — forward on a ground ball, back/pause on a fly ball.

      **Setup:** Runner at first in secondary lead position. Partner stands 5–10 feet away in the runner's line of sight and calls either "ground!" or "fly!" at random intervals.

      **Steps:**
      1. Runner holds secondary lead position, ready to react.
      2. Partner calls out ball type:
         - **"Ground!"** — Runner immediately drives toward second with a crossover step. First step is with the right foot crossing over to the left.
         - **"Fly!"** — Runner retreats toward first with a drop step. First step is back with the left foot.
      3. Runner commits fully to the move for 3–4 steps, then resets.
      4. Partner can delay the call or give a fake (call "ground" quietly, then immediately say "fly") to increase the challenge.

      **Reps:** 20 calls. Mix ratio roughly 60% ground, 40% fly.

      <Warning>When practicing the fly-ball retreat step, make sure the runner returns fully to the bag before resetting — don't let short-cutting the drill build the habit of not getting fully back on a real pickoff.</Warning>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Read-and-React from First — Live or Soft-Toss BP">
      **Purpose:** Apply secondary lead timing and first-step reaction to a real batted ball in a low-pressure BP environment.

      **Setup:** Runner at first, hitter at home plate taking soft-toss or front-toss BP. A coach stands near second base to give send/hold calls as a check — but the runner should make their own read first, then confirm with the coach.

      **Steps:**
      1. Runner takes primary lead and secondary shuffles as normal at pitch delivery.
      2. When the ball is hit, the runner reads:
         - **Hard ground ball through the right side**: go hard to second, read third.
         - **Ground ball to the left side (shortstop/third baseman)**: hold — you may be doubled off.
         - **Fly ball**: freeze, read if it will be caught, then re-evaluate.
         - **Line drive**: freeze first — if caught, you may be doubled off the base.
      3. Coach at second calls out "right read" or "wrong read" after the runner commits.
      4. Discuss every wrong read immediately. Don't move to the next rep until the runner can verbalize why the read was wrong.

      **Reps:** 20–25 batted balls.
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Tag-Up Timing — Leave at Catch">
      **Purpose:** Build the precise habit of tagging up on a fly ball — leaving the base the instant the fielder catches the ball, not before and not a full second after.

      **Setup:** Runner at second or third base. Feeder tosses fly balls to an outfielder (or partner) at medium depth. Runner watches the ball.

      **Steps:**
      1. Runner stands on the base with one foot touching the bag, ready to push off.
      2. Watch the outfielder's glove — not the ball's arc. The glove tells you when the catch is about to happen.
      3. The instant the glove closes on the ball, push off and sprint toward the next base.
      4. A coach with a stopwatch times from catch to the runner's crossing of the next bag.
      5. If you're tagging early (before the catch): that's the most common mistake at this level. Use the glove-focus technique — glove closes, you go.

      **Reps:** 12 tag-up reps from third (easier read), 8 from second (harder — longer throw).

      <Note>Tag-up timing from third is one of the highest-value skills in all of baseball. A well-executed tag on a medium-depth fly scores a run that lazy runners leave on base every time.</Note>
    </Accordion>
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Intermediate">
    At the Intermediate level you've developed the reads and the footwork. Now you'll train against simulated pressure — pickoff moves, live-BP sends and holds, steal technique, and high-leverage contact-play decisions.

    <Accordion title="Lead-Off and Get-Back Drill — Simulated Pickoff">
      **Purpose:** Train your response to a pickoff move so it becomes automatic and disciplined — not panicked and slow.

      **Setup:** Runner at first. Partner (or coach) plays the role of the pitcher on a mound or marked spot. Partner will alternate between delivering to home and throwing a simulated pickoff to first.

      **Steps:**
      1. Runner takes primary lead — 2–3 shuffle steps. Weight balanced, ready to go either direction.
      2. Pitcher reads: watch the pitcher's front shoulder and feet.
         - If the pitcher's **front shoulder turns toward first** or their **foot pivot starts toward first** — that's the pickoff move. Drop step with your left foot, drive back to the bag hard.
         - If the shoulder turns toward home — secondary shuffle begins normally.
      3. Tag the bag with your right hand as you dive or slide back. Stay low.
      4. Partner mixes pickoff attempts randomly — at least 30–40% of reps should be pickoffs to keep the runner honest.

      **Reps:** 20 reps total (at least 6–8 simulated pickoffs mixed in).

      <Note>The biggest mistake on pickoff reads is watching the ball instead of the pitcher's body. The ball moves after the decision is already made. The pitcher's body telegraphs the decision early — train your eyes to read the body, not the ball.</Note>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="First-Step Steal Technique — Crossover, Stay Low for 3 Steps">
      **Purpose:** Develop the correct mechanical sequence for a stolen base attempt — a crossover first step, low body position through the first three strides, and a proper slide entry.

      **Setup:** First base, 90 feet of running lane. Partner or coach watches mechanics from behind (down the base line).

      **Steps:**
      1. Take your primary lead — maximize distance while staying under control and able to get back.
      2. On the steal "go" signal (or pitcher's release moment):
         - **Crossover step**: right foot crosses over the left foot toward second. This is faster than a jab step.
         - **Stay low**: first 3 strides are driven aggressively forward with a slight forward lean — not upright.
         - **Drive phase**: strides 4–8, accelerate to full speed. Head up, eyes on second.
      3. Approach the bag prepared to slide: pop-up slide (most common), hook slide (away from the throw), or headfirst (only if trained properly and appropriate).
      4. Do 5 reps of just the first 3 steps slowly, checking the crossover and body position, then build to full-speed 90-foot steals.

      **Reps:** 5 slow-motion (first 3 steps only), then 8–10 full-speed steal attempts.

      <Warning>Headfirst slides into first base are generally not recommended — they are slower than running through the bag and carry higher injury risk to fingers and wrists. Use a pop-up or hook slide into second and third.</Warning>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Read Live Batted Balls in Live BP">
      **Purpose:** Apply all your reads — ground ball, fly ball, line drive, tag-up — against unpredictable live BP hitting.

      **Setup:** Full infield/outfield setup if possible. Runner at first (or second). Hitter taking live BP or front-toss BP. Coach observes runner decisions.

      **Steps:**
      1. Runner takes primary and secondary lead on each pitch.
      2. React to every batted ball in real time — no one tells you what to do.
      3. After each play, coach gives a one-sentence read evaluation: "Correct — you went on the ground ball to the right side" or "Wrong — that line drive was almost caught, you should have frozen."
      4. Rotate runners every 10–12 batted balls so each runner gets frequent repetitions.
      5. Log each runner's correct-read percentage across the session. Target: 80%+ correct reads.

      **Reps:** 12–15 batted-ball reads per runner. Run 2–3 runners simultaneously if field setup allows.
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Contact-Play Decision Drill — Runner on Third, Fewer Than 2 Outs">
      **Purpose:** Develop the ability to execute the contact play (go on contact, regardless of ball type) or hold based on depth and trajectory of the batted ball.

      **Setup:** Runner on third, hitter at home taking front-toss BP, coach observing from behind third base.

      **Contact Play Scenario (0–1 outs, infield back):**
      1. On contact — any ground ball — go home immediately. Don't wait. The fielder's job is to make the throw; your job is to score.
      2. On a fly ball hit shallow: freeze and tag if caught. Go if it falls in.
      3. On a fly ball hit deep to the outfield: tag up and score on the catch.

      **Half-Way Read Scenario (infield in — used when fielders are drawn up):**
      1. On a ground ball directly at an infielder: freeze — they may catch it and throw home.
      2. On a ground ball through the infield: go immediately.
      3. On a fly ball: freeze at halfway, read if it's caught, then go or return.

      **Steps:**
      1. Coach calls "infield in" or "infield back" before each pitch so the runner knows which scenario applies.
      2. Runner makes their own decision and executes.
      3. Coach evaluates after each rep.

      **Reps:** 15–20 contact-play reads.

      <Note>The contact play from third is one of the most game-impactful plays in baseball and one of the most poorly executed at the amateur level. Drilling it repeatedly is worth every minute invested.</Note>
    </Accordion>
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Advanced">
    Advanced base runners have the reads, the mechanics, and the instincts. The work at this level is about sharpening timing with film, executing against live pitching, staying sharp under game-speed pressure, and running full simulated half-innings where every decision compounds.

    <Accordion title="Timed Sprints with Technique Review — Film from the Side">
      **Purpose:** Maintain and improve sprint mechanics specifically applied to base-running situations, using film to catch technical drift.

      **Setup:** Camera on tripod, filming from the side at first base. Time all sprints with a stopwatch or radar app. Film every session.

      **Sprint 1 — Home to First (Swing Break):**
      1. Full swing simulation from the batter's box. Break at swing completion.
      2. Run full speed through the bag, turning right.
      3. Target time: under 4.3 seconds (right-handed), under 4.2 (left-handed) for the 90-foot distance.

      **Sprint 2 — First to Third:**
      1. Secondary lead position at first. "Go" signal from coach.
      2. Round second properly, drive to third. Take a pop-up slide.
      3. Film from the third-base side. Look for: does the runner hit the inside corner of second, and are they at full speed through the corner?

      **Technique Review:**
      - Watch the side-angle film after each sprint block.
      - Specifically check: forward lean in acceleration phase, knee drive height, whether the inside corners of the bases are being hit.
      - Log one technical note per session.

      **Reps:** 4–6 sprints per type. Full rest between.

      <Note>Sprint quality drops significantly after 6 high-intensity reps. More reps at lower effort is not better — keep volume controlled and intensity maximal.</Note>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Secondary Leads Against Live Pitching">
      **Purpose:** Execute perfect secondary lead timing against a live pitcher who is trying to hold you on and generate pickoff attempts — the closest simulation to a real game.

      **Setup:** Live pitcher on a mound (or a partner throwing at 70–80% velocity), catcher behind home. Runner at first.

      **Steps:**
      1. Runner takes their full primary lead. The pitcher is allowed to throw over to first at any time.
      2. On every pitch to home, the runner takes two secondary shuffle steps at the moment of release — not early.
      3. Coach times the secondary lead and calls out whether the timing was correct ("on release," "early," or "late").
      4. After 5 pitches, the coach can call a "steal" — the runner attempts a real steal on the next pitch using proper crossover mechanics.
      5. Pitcher is encouraged to throw over 20–30% of the time to keep the runner honest.

      **Reps:** 3 rounds of 8–10 pitches each. At least one steal attempt per round.

      <Warning>During live pitching drills, the catcher must be in full gear. Keep all bystanders behind the backstop or in designated safe areas. Establish a clear "dead ball" signal before the drill begins.</Warning>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Send/Hold Reads with Coach Feedback — Third-Base Coach Simulation">
      **Purpose:** Execute send-or-hold decisions from a third-base coaching perspective, simulating the high-pressure reads that happen multiple times per game.

      **Setup:** Runner at first or second. Coach acts as third-base coach with full authority to wave, hold, or send. Hitter at home taking live or machine BP.

      **Steps:**
      1. Runner runs the bases as they would in a game. Third-base coach makes all decisions and communicates with signals (wave, hold with both hands up, etc.) — no verbal calls.
      2. Runner must read the third-base coach while running at full speed and rounding the base — not slowing down to look.
      3. After each play, coach and runner debrief: "When did you pick up my signal? Were you already committed before you saw me?"
      4. Work on rounding second at full speed and picking up the coach at the far edge of the coaching box — this is the key visual checkpoint.

      **Reps:** 20 batted-ball reps per runner.

      <Note>The third-base coach's signal must be read *at* the second-base bag, not after passing it. If you're reading the coach after you've rounded second, you're already 1–2 steps behind a correct read.</Note>
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Full Base-Running Simulation — Half-Inning">
      **Purpose:** Put every base-running skill together in a continuous, game-speed half-inning simulation where multiple situations compound and decisions have consequences.

      **Setup:** Full field if available. Pitcher, hitter, fielders (even partial). 3–4 runners rotate through. Coach tracks correct and incorrect decisions throughout.

      **Format:**
      1. Run a full half-inning with live or machine BP. Runners are placed on the appropriate bases after each at-bat result.
      2. Every runner makes all their own decisions — secondary leads, reads, tag-ups, contact plays. No one is told what to do.
      3. Coach observes silently, logging every base-running decision.
      4. After the half-inning, debrief in under 5 minutes: call out 2–3 decisions that were correct, 1–2 that were wrong, and why.
      5. Run a second half-inning with the corrections in mind.

      **Cadence:** 2 half-innings per full base-running session, once per week minimum.

      <Note>The debrief is the most important part of this drill. Game-speed mistakes that are never discussed become game-speed habits. Keep the debrief tight and specific — call the actual play and the correct decision, not general concepts.</Note>
    </Accordion>
  </Tab>
