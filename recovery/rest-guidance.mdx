---
title: "In-Season Rest Guidance for Baseball Players"
sidebarTitle: "Rest Guidance"
description: "Skill-level rest day targets, sleep recommendations, and arm care habits to keep baseball players healthy through a full competitive season."
---

Staying healthy through a full season takes more than icing your arm after games. It requires building real rest into your weekly schedule before fatigue and soreness start compounding. The guidance below is organized by skill level because what a nine-year-old's arm needs during a ten-game rec league season is genuinely different from what a seventeen-year-old needs during a 70-game travel ball summer. Find your level, read the habits, and build them into your routine — not just the weeks when you feel worn down.

<Note>
  **This is general guidance, not medical advice.** Pain in the shoulder or elbow — especially sharp, deep, or persistent pain — is not normal soreness. Stop throwing and see a doctor or certified athletic trainer. Do not attempt to train or play through joint pain.
</Note>

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Beginner">
    ## Beginner — Ages ~7–10

    At this stage, the priority is keeping the game fun and keeping the arm healthy. Throwing volume is naturally low at this level, but that doesn't mean rest habits don't matter — the patterns you build now carry forward.

    ### Rest days
    Take **at least 2 full rest days per week**, ideally spread across the week rather than back-to-back. If your athlete plays a weekend doubleheader, build rest into the early part of the following week.

    ### Sleep
    Kids in this age range need **9–11 hours of sleep per night**. Sleep is when the body does the majority of its tissue repair and growth. It's not optional recovery — it's the most important recovery tool available.

    ### Arm pain
    **Never play through shoulder or elbow pain.** At this age, pain in those joints can signal stress on growth plates. Growth plate injuries in young athletes can have long-term consequences if ignored. If a child complains of arm pain, stop throwing immediately and have them evaluated.

    <Warning>
      Beginner-level athletes should never be pushed to throw through pain by coaches, parents, or peer pressure. "Shake it off" is not appropriate advice for a 7–10 year old with elbow or shoulder pain.
    </Warning>

    ### Habits to build
    - **Warm up before throwing.** Even at a catch session, a 5-minute dynamic warm-up (arm circles, hip rotations, light jog) makes a difference.
    - **Don't throw hard every day.** Most throwing at this age should be easy and relaxed — learning movement patterns, not testing limits.
    - **If the arm feels tired, stop.** Fatigue changes mechanics and is when most youth arm injuries happen.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Developing">
    ## Developing — Ages ~11–14

    This is the age range where throwing volume increases significantly and the competitive calendar starts to get serious. It's also when growth plates in the elbow and shoulder are most vulnerable. Rest habits become genuinely important here, not just good practice.

    ### Rest days
    Take **1–2 full rest days per week that include real arm rest**. "Real arm rest" means no throwing at all — not even light catch. If you're playing a heavy weekend schedule (two games Saturday, one Sunday), Monday and Tuesday should be arm-off days.

    ### Sleep
    Aim for **9–10 hours per night**. Athletes in early adolescence are growing rapidly, and sleep drives growth hormone release. Chronic under-sleep at this age compounds fatigue faster than it does in adults.

    ### Growth spurts
    During periods of rapid growth — typically identifiable by soreness in the long bones of the arms and legs, or a sudden increase in height — **be more conservative with throwing volume**, not less. Growing bone is more susceptible to stress. If an athlete has grown two inches in the past three months, treat them like a beginner in terms of arm load until they've stabilized.

    <Tip>
      A useful rule of thumb during growth spurts: cut throwing volume by roughly 25% and avoid max-effort throwing until the growth phase levels off. Keep mechanics work going — just reduce the load.
    </Tip>

    ### Habits to build
    - **Track pitch counts if pitching.** Use your league's pitch count limits as a floor, not a ceiling.
    - **Separate "arm rest" from "rest day."** A rest day where you do lower-body conditioning is fine. But the arm needs to come off completely on rest days.
    - **Communicate fatigue to coaches.** Developing athletes often don't want to show weakness — make sure they understand that reporting arm fatigue is smart, not soft.

    <Warning>
      "Little League Elbow" (medial apophysitis) is common in this age group and is almost always caused by overuse. If an athlete reports medial (inner) elbow pain after throwing, rest immediately and seek evaluation. This is not something to wait out.
    </Warning>
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Intermediate">
    ## Intermediate — Ages ~14–16

    At the intermediate level, training gets structured and the season gets long. High school and competitive travel programs can run March through August with almost no breaks. Managing cumulative fatigue — not just single-session soreness — is the skill that keeps athletes healthy through a full calendar.

    ### Rest days
    Take **at least 1 full rest day per week**. This is the minimum, not the target. There will be weeks with double-headers and back-to-back games where that one rest day is hard to fit in — protect it anyway.

    ### Arm rest vs. body rest
    **Separate arm rest days from full body rest days.** An intermediate athlete can do lower-body strength work, sprints, or conditioning on days when the arm is resting. But understand that cardio-only days are still contributing to overall fatigue — they're not genuinely restorative. At least one day per week should be low-stress for the whole body.

    ### Sleep
    Target **8–10 hours per night**. Most high school athletes are sleeping 6–7 hours because of early school start times. This is a real problem — chronic sleep debt degrades recovery, reaction time, and mood. If you can't hit 8 hours at night, a 20-minute nap after school meaningfully helps.

    ### Deload weeks
    Build **deload weeks into multi-week training blocks**. Every 3–4 weeks of elevated training load, take a week where throwing volume drops by 30–40% and intensity stays moderate. This isn't a rest week — you're still training, just giving the body a chance to absorb the previous block. Athletes who train without deloads consistently eventually break down.

    <Accordion title="What does a deload week actually look like?">
      A deload week for an intermediate pitcher might look like: 2 light bullpen sessions at 70% effort instead of 3 at full intensity, no max-effort work, regular lower-body and core training, and at least 2 arm-off days. The goal is to maintain movement patterns while reducing cumulative stress on the arm.
    </Accordion>

    ### Habits to build
    - **Monitor how your arm feels across a week, not just after each session.** If it's sore at the start of a throwing session — before you've even warmed up — that's a signal to back off.
    - **Ice after high-effort outings** (games, heavy bullpens) as a standard habit, not just when you feel pain.
    - **Communicate with your coach about arm fatigue.** Intermediate athletes are old enough to self-report accurately — use that.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Advanced">
    ## Advanced — Ages ~16–18+

    Advanced athletes are operating at high intensity over a long competitive calendar. The training is serious, the stakes feel higher, and the temptation to push through soreness is real. The habits that keep advanced players healthy aren't about doing less — they're about being precise and deliberate with workload management across months, not days.

    ### Rest days
    Take **1 planned full rest day per week, every week**. The emphasis is on *planned* — built into your schedule in advance, not something that happens because you're too sore to show up. If the week's schedule makes a full rest day impossible, protect arm rest above everything else.

    ### Sleep
    Aim for **7–9 hours per night**. High school schedules, early practices, and weekend travel make this hard. Treat sleep with the same discipline you'd bring to a weight training session — it's a performance input, not optional downtime.

    ### Track fatigue seasonally
    **Monitor fatigue across a season, not just day to day.** The arm that felt fine in April might be running on empty in July after three months of games, bullpens, and showcase weekends. Keep a simple log — even just a 1–5 fatigue rating each morning — so you can see trends before they become injuries.

    <Tip>
      A fatigue log doesn't need to be complicated. A note in your phone with the date and a number (1 = fresh, 5 = really worn down) takes ten seconds and gives you data to act on. If you're averaging 3.5+ over two consecutive weeks, something needs to change in your load.
    </Tip>

    ### Deliberate lighter days
    **Use lighter days deliberately, not as a default.** An advanced athlete who only throws at 100% or not at all is missing a tool. Deliberately programmed moderate-effort days — 70% on a 4-seam, flat-ground work on secondary pitches — keep the arm moving, reinforce mechanics, and allow recovery without a full shutdown. These aren't "easy days" because you're tired. They're intentional low-load sessions built into the plan.

    ### Habits to build
    - **Distinguish between muscle fatigue and joint pain.** Arm soreness in the belly of the bicep or forearm muscle after a long outing is normal. Pain in the elbow joint, at the UCL (inner elbow), or in the shoulder joint is not — stop and get it evaluated.
    - **Manage showcase and showcase-adjacent workload.** Showcases are high-effort, low-rest events that add up fast in the summer. Account for them when planning your surrounding week.
    - **Build a pre-season baseline.** Before the season starts, know what "normal" feels like for your arm. That baseline is what you compare against all season long.

    <Warning>
      UCL stress and early UCL damage are most common in 16–18 year old pitchers — exactly this age group. The #1 risk factor is accumulated overuse across multiple seasons without adequate recovery. Take rest seriously now, when it's preventive. The alternative is often a significant injury that costs a full season or more.
    </Warning>
  </Tab>
</Tabs>
